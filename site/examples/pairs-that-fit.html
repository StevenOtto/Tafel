The pairs that fit

One morning in February 1953, James Watson pushed cardboard cut-outs of DNA's four bases around a desk in Cambridge until two of the pairs came out the same shape. This page lets you push them around yourself.

1What was found

DNA is two strands wound around each other. Each strand is a backbone carrying a string of bases, and there are four kinds: adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C) and thymine (T). A and G are purines, with two fused rings. C and T are pyrimidines, with one. The strands hold together where a base on one strand meets a base on the other.

The finding: A pairs only with T, and G only with C. Each correct pair is one purine plus one pyrimidine, so both pairs have almost exactly the same overall width. That is what lets a perfectly regular helix carry any sequence at all.

Try all sixteen combinations. Only two fit. The rails are the two backbones, drawn at the one width the double helix allows. Filled dots are hydrogens a base offers to its partner, open rings are atoms that can accept one.

    3How the pairs hold

    A hydrogen bond forms when a hydrogen attached to a nitrogen or oxygen (a donor) points at a nitrogen or oxygen with a free pair of electrons (an acceptor). Two donors facing each other repel. Two acceptors facing each other have nothing to share. Each base presents a fixed pattern of donors and acceptors along its pairing edge, and a pair holds only if the two patterns interlock.

    Where the hydrogens sit is the subtle part. G and T each exist in two forms, called tautomers, that differ in whether a hydrogen sits on a ring nitrogen (the keto form) or on an oxygen (the enol form). The textbooks Watson used drew the enol forms. Donohue, who knew the crystal structures of related small molecules, told him the keto forms were the right ones.

    Switch between the two and watch the table. Under the textbook forms, none of the sixteen combinations holds on this edge. Under Donohue's forms exactly two do: A-T with two hydrogen bonds and G-C with three.

    4Why it matters

    Because every correct pair has the same width, the sequence along a strand is unconstrained. The helix does not care what it carries, so it can carry information.

    Because A always faces T and G always faces C, Chargaff's ratios follow automatically. Tap any base on the top strand to change it, and watch the counts.

    Top strand, read left to right. The partner strand below runs in the opposite direction.

    And because each strand fixes the other completely, a cell can separate the two and build a new partner for each. Watson and Crick's 1953 paper closed by noting, in one deliberately understated sentence, that the pairing suggested a copying mechanism. Cover the partner strand, predict each base, then tap to check. You will not need any information beyond the top strand.

    1. 1947

      Gulland and Jordan's titrations of DNA show that most of its bases are hydrogen-bonded to one another.

    2. 1950

      Chargaff publishes his base ratios: in DNA from many species, A roughly equals T and G roughly equals C.

    3. May 1952

      Franklin and Gosling record Photo 51, an X-ray pattern of the hydrated B form of DNA.

    4. 30 January 1953

      Wilkins shows Watson Photo 51. Its cross-shaped pattern signals a helix.

    5. Late February 1953

      Watson builds a like-with-like model, pairing A with A and G with G, using the textbook forms. The Double Helix includes a schematic of this version.

    6. A day later

      Donohue objects: the textbook forms of G and T are almost certainly wrong.

    7. 28 February 1953

      Using cardboard bases in Donohue's forms, Watson finds that A-T, held by two hydrogen bonds, has the same shape as G-C, held by three.

    8. 25 April 1953

      Nature publishes Watson and Crick's one-page letter, alongside papers by Wilkins, Stokes and Wilson and by Franklin and Gosling.

    9. 16 April 1958

      Franklin dies at 37.

    10. 1962

      Crick, Watson and Wilkins receive the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The prize is not awarded posthumously.

    What this page simplifies

    Each pairing edge is reduced to three sites, and the shapes are schematic, not measured. The real A-T and G-C pairs differ in width by a fraction of an angstrom, not zero. Watson's like-with-like pairs used a different edge of the bases than the one modelled here, so the bench shows why they fail on width, not the exact bonds he drew. Other pairings, such as the G-T wobble pair, do occur in RNA and as copying errors, with shifted geometry this model does not capture. Each person in the second section contributed far more than the one part highlighted.
